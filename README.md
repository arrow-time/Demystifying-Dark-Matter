# Demystifying Dark Matter

Dark matter is not a particle but a low-compression locked state of space from the early universe, formed by coupling between network spinors and fixed-chirality SEQ spins. It gravitates but does not interact via nuclear forces.

What is dark matter? Astrophysicists tell us that the anomaly in galaxy rotation curves—the fact that peripheral stars orbit far faster than can be explained by the gravity of visible matter—implies the existence of some invisible mass in the universe. It neither emits light nor interacts with electromagnetic waves, yet its gravitational effects are undeniable. For decades, physicists have proposed candidates ranging from weakly interacting massive particles to axions, but no dark matter particle has ever been directly detected in any laboratory. This paper offers a radically different answer: dark matter is not a particle, but a low-compression locked state of space.

To understand dark matter, we must first recall the core mechanism from the previous article. In this paper's framework, ordinary matter is a stable compressed state formed when space is strongly compressed by SU(3) and locked by the Higgs chiral lock. This compression occurs at subnuclear scales with an enormous compression ratio, storing extremely high energy density—this is the origin of ordinary (baryonic) matter mass. But compressed states are not all the same. The degree of compression can vary, and the locking mechanism can have different strengths.

The formation of dark matter is closely tied to the evolutionary history of the early universe. The paper divides cosmic evolution into multiple stages. In the first stage (the early period of cosmic expansion), the entire universe was in a highly compressed state. At this time, spacetime network spinors were highly concentrated in certain local regions. When high compression coexists with a high-intensity spinor field, the synergy between SU(3) compression and Higgs locking efficiently produces ordinary matter—that is, high-compression locked states.

But dark matter formed at a slightly later stage. As the universe expanded, the overall degree of spatial compression gradually decreased. At some critical point, the compression level became insufficient to trigger the SU(3) compression mechanism—the compression ratio was too small to store energy in the form of a QCD spring array. However, spacetime network spinors still existed, though with diminished strength. At this stage, if some local spatial compression remained and the spinor field still possessed some intensity, the two could still couple and lock, forming a compressed state—only with a compression degree far lower than that of ordinary matter. This is dark matter.

In other words, dark matter and ordinary matter share the same physical mechanism: both are spatial compressed states locked by the coupling between spinors and fixed-chirality spins. The only difference lies in the degree of compression. Ordinary matter is the product of high-compression locking; dark matter is the product of low-compression locking. Just as the same spring can be locked by strong compression or caught by mild compression—both are locked states, but the stored energy and the resulting external stretching effect are dramatically different.

This framework explains several observed characteristics of dark matter. First, dark matter does not participate in electromagnetic interactions. In this paper's framework, electromagnetic interactions are associated with SEQ resonance and spin; as a low-compression state, dark matter has different SEQ resonant frequencies and spin excitation modes from ordinary matter, insufficient to produce observable electromagnetic radiation. Second, dark matter does not participate in strong interactions. The SU(3) compression mechanism of QCD requires a certain compression threshold to be activated; dark matter's compression degree lies below this threshold, so it does not participate in nuclear reactions. Third, dark matter still generates gravity. Any spatial compressed state—regardless of its compression degree—induces stretching of external space, which is gravity. Dark matter's gravitational effect is identical to that of ordinary matter, depending only on the total amount of compression (i.e., equivalent mass), not on the microscopic compression mechanism. Fourth, dark matter is more diffusely distributed in the universe than ordinary matter. Because it formed at a later stage with lower compression, dark matter tends to be distributed over the large-scale halos of galaxies rather than condensing into dense objects.

This framework also explains the observed ratio between dark matter and ordinary matter. In the early universe, highly compressed regions preferentially formed ordinary matter. As the universe expanded and entropy increased, the degree of compression decreased, and the total amount of dark matter subsequently formed could exceed that of ordinary matter. This is broadly consistent with the observation that dark matter constitutes approximately 85% of the total mass of the universe.

More importantly, this framework makes testable predictions about dark matter. Since dark matter is a compressed state of space rather than some unknown particle, it will not appear as a new particle in collider experiments. Furthermore, the distribution of dark matter should be directly correlated with the distribution of spacetime network spinors in the early universe—meaning the structure of dark matter halos may carry fossil information about the initial conditions of the universe. Future gravitational wave detectors or galaxy surveys may reveal this correlation.

In summary, dark matter is not an "extension" of the Standard Model of particle physics, but a different excitation mode of the spatial elastic network itself. Ordinary matter is a high-compression locked state of space; dark matter is a low-compression locked state of space. They share the same origin and same structure, differing only in compression degree. This reduces dark matter from "mysterious particle" to "a state of space," explaining dark matter's gravitational effects and its lack of electromagnetic and strong interactions without introducing any new particles.
